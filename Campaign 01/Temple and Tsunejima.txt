Temple Home and Tsunejima

The repository contains a dedicated consolidated canon file, Temple Grounds.txt, which already gathers the temple and island geography developed across the campaign. The description below uses that file as the primary source, while separating explicit canon from connective spatial reconstruction.

1. Overall shape of Tsunejima

Tsunejima is a roughly circular artificial island created and anchored by Jayson. The original temple and its surrounding village were translocated onto it and rooted into the real world rather than merely copied or rebuilt. The temple settlement now occupies a hidden valley near the island’s centre.

The island is not a single uniform landscape. It contains deliberately interwoven regions of:

temperate woodland
plains and moss meadows
jungle
desert
high mountains
rivers
waterfall-fed ravines
coastal cliffs

The biomes are engineered to interact. Ley-winds and artificial or guided watercourses circulate heat, rain, mist, and moisture from one region to another, allowing dramatically different environments to exist on the same island without feeling like disconnected boxes.

The central valley is wrapped in ancient forest bands and crossed by crystalline streams. Within the valley are natural elevation changes, stone paths, sacred ponds, canopy bridges and high ridge perches. The result is not a flat temple compound. It is a layered settlement built across slopes, streambeds, terraces and wooded rises.

Reconstructed island arrangement

The most coherent physical arrangement is:

Centre: temple, central courtyard and oldest sacred grounds
Around the temple: the original village and its later residential expansion
Outer valley: farmland, specialist neighbourhoods, forest routes and training areas
Southern corridor: South Gate, primary stone road, harbour quarter and Grand Dock
East and west corridors: agricultural terraces, supply entrances and ceremonial approaches
Outer island: interwoven biomes, mountains, rivers, ravines and coastline
Coast: cliffs, beaches or harbour terraces, beacon obelisks and island access points

This radial arrangement is partly inferred, but it follows the explicitly stated relationship between the central hidden valley, the gates, farmland, southern harbour and surrounding biomes.

2. The hidden valley

The temple home sits in the valley at Tsunejima’s heart. It is enclosed by layered Veil barriers and broad rings of ancient forest. Outsiders cannot find or enter it without permission carried through the soul-thread system. The Veil does not merely recognise identity. It reacts to intent.

Approaching the valley from the outer island, the landscape would gradually become calmer and more cultivated. Dense forest routes would open into mossy clearings, stream crossings and old stone approaches. Waystones and softly glowing path-lines would guide approved travellers toward the gates while leading hostile or unauthorised travellers nowhere useful.

The valley floor is divided naturally by water and elevation. Streams descend from the surrounding ridges and waterfalls, feeding ponds, koi channels, irrigation terraces and narrow ravines before continuing toward the coast. Buildings are therefore likely arranged on dry rises and terraces rather than imposed in a rigid street grid.

The landscape is spiritual infrastructure as much as scenery. Paths glow during vows and rites. Lanterns flicker in response to nearby emotional states. The land itself can brighten during bonding, dim during communal sorrow and produce a singing resonance during powerful thread events. Hidden mirrors around the grounds show soul-thread forms instead of ordinary reflections.

3. The temple’s primary approach
South Gate and harbour road

The principal practical entrance is the South Gate. It stands at the end of the main stone road running north from the Grand Dock. The gate is a reinforced torii fitted with resonance inlays linked to the WOK and the wider defensive network. It serves both as the ceremonial southern threshold and as the main route for cargo, visitors and organised arrivals.

A traveller arriving by ship would move through this sequence:

Harbour terraces and dock
Guest processing and truth-veil inspection
Harbour quarter
Long stone approach road
Outer forest and agricultural lands
South Gate
Village avenues
Temple precinct

The road would be broad enough for carts, supply vehicles and ceremonial processions. Beacon obelisks along it guide harbour-to-temple convoys and integrate with the island’s non-lethal defensive field.

East Gate

The East Gate is a more decorative torii-style entrance bearing fox motifs. It is used for honoured guests, caravans, formal rites and ceremonial arrivals. Rather than leading directly to the industrial side of the settlement, it likely opens onto a landscaped procession path through flowering trees, farmland and prayer gardens.

West Gate

The West Gate is the practical trade and supply entrance. It connects to farmland and hidden routes through the western woods. Supplies not arriving by sea could enter here without crossing the central ceremonial areas.

Forest trails

Additional north-south forest trails are used by veilrunners and for discreet travel. These are narrower routes marked by waystones linked to the obelisk network. They provide movement around or through the valley without forcing every traveller onto the main roads.

4. Central temple precinct

The original temple forms the sacred and architectural heart of the settlement. It is best understood as a collection of connected halls, courtyards, gardens and specialist chambers rather than one enormous building.

Central courtyard

The central courtyard is the settlement’s primary open ceremonial space. At its centre stands the WOK, a monolith twenty metres tall but only two metres in radius. Its narrow, towering proportions make it resemble a dark or polished needle rising above the surrounding tiled roofs.

The WOK generates a field that excludes hatred and malice. It also functions as a truth-resonance mechanism during judgment and synchronises with the island’s obelisk network.

The courtyard would therefore be both beautiful and slightly uncanny. Someone entering with peaceful intent would feel little beyond warmth, pressure or a low resonance. Someone carrying active hatred would encounter resistance before reaching the heart of the compound.

Around the courtyard are likely:

formal stone paving
ritual circles
lantern posts
flame-marked lineage tiles
covered walkways
access to the main shrine hall
children’s teaching circles
smaller routes toward the gardens and residences

The ceremonial tailmarks are flame-kissed tiles recording lineage and important passages. These may form borders, pathways or concentric bands around the courtyard and shrine approaches.

Main Shrine Hall

The Main Shrine Hall houses the Eternal Flame Altar and is used for rituals and offerings. It would naturally occupy the dominant position beyond the central courtyard, probably slightly elevated on a stone or timber platform.

Its likely visual character is an enlarged Japanese shrine form adapted to Kitsune culture:

deep overhanging eaves
curved rooflines
exposed timber
carved fox and flame motifs
broad entrance stairs
hanging wards or thread-banners
warm internal light from the Eternal Flame
open side galleries overlooking water or gardens

The Eternal Flame would act as the visual anchor. Most temple paths and sightlines would subtly point toward it, even where trees or buildings partially obscure the hall.

Inner Sanctuary

Behind or within the Main Shrine Hall is the restricted Inner Sanctuary. It is reserved for Matrons and High Threads and is used for soul-weaving and communion.

This space would be quieter, smaller and more enclosed than the public hall. It is likely separated by screens, warded doors, curtain-like thread barriers or a descending passage into the oldest portion of the temple.

Weaving Chamber

The Weaving Chamber lies beneath the temple’s west eaves. It contains ward-silk looms and the Matron’s thread-altar. Its stated position indicates that it is attached directly to the sacred complex rather than standing as a separate workshop.

The room probably sits partly below ground or against the western slope, giving it stable temperature and subdued light. Shelves, suspended threads, ritual spindles and looms would fill the chamber. The west-facing location also places it nearer the practical and agricultural side of the settlement.

Thread Courtyard

Children’s lessons and tail-ritual instruction occur in class circles within a Thread Courtyard under living lanterns.

This is probably a secondary courtyard connected to the main temple but sheltered from major processions. The circles may be marked into stone or packed earth, with low seating and enough open space for physical instruction. It would be active during the day and softly luminous in the evening.

Watch-perch balcony

A high balcony faces the ridge and is used for night watch and sky-reading. It likely extends from an upper temple hall or one of the raised residential wings.

From it, a watcher could see:

the temple roofs below
lanterns winding through the village
the dark forest ring
the higher ridge perches
parts of the island beyond the valley
stars unobstructed by ordinary urban light
5. Temple gardens and water

Water appears throughout the temple home. It is not limited to one decorative pond. Streams, pools, waterfalls and irrigation channels bind the entire valley together.

Prayer gardens

The prayer gardens contain koi streams, fox effigies and flowering trees. They probably form a transition zone between the formal shrine precinct and the quieter natural grounds.

Stone paths would cross the streams by small arched bridges or flat stepping stones. Fox statues or carved effigies would sit at bends, shrine niches and tree roots. Flowering trees would provide strong seasonal changes, turning the gardens from blossom-filled ceremonial spaces into shaded summer retreats and then into red-gold autumn corridors.

Memory Pools

The Memory Pools are reflective bodies of water that preserve echoes of past threads. They are spiritual anchors, not merely ornamental ponds.

They would suit a secluded section behind or beside the shrine complex, with restricted paths and places to kneel at the water’s edge. Their surfaces may remain unnaturally still even when nearby streams move.

Ayaka’s koi pond

Ayaka’s koi pond is explicitly a meditation and reflection area.

This pond should feel orderly and composed:

carefully positioned stones
trimmed plants
clear water
koi visible beneath the surface
a small sitting platform or flat bank
direct but quiet access from the temple or residence

It likely lies close enough to the main grounds for regular visits but far enough away that temple activity fades into the background.

Seika’s hideaway pond

Seika’s pond is associated with joy and comfort rather than formal meditation.

It is likely less manicured than Ayaka’s pond and tucked deeper among trees, reeds or flower-covered banks. A curved side trail, concealed opening or narrow bridge would make it feel private without truly being remote. It may contain informal seating, overhanging branches and places to lie beside the water.

Waterfall caverns

The waterfall caverns are used for spiritual trials and private rites.

They likely occupy one of the ravines beyond the immediate temple grounds. Water descending from the mountain zone enters caves or cuts through dark stone chambers. Access would be by steep steps, narrow paths or a trail following the stream uphill.

Bloom arches

Bloom arches are seasonal flower-covered bridges crossing streams.

These could be living trellises or deliberately trained trees forming arched crossings. They would appear throughout the residential and sacred garden districts, preventing the stream network from dividing the settlement into isolated pockets.

6. The village

The village has expanded to approximately 1,500 homes, woven into the temple’s visual language rather than forming a separate modern suburb. Growth corridors remain available for later expansion.

Each house contains:

a tailhook shrine
a glowplate lantern
a wayline connecting it to the nearest obelisk node

This gives the residential districts a consistent spiritual and infrastructural identity.

The houses are best imagined in clustered neighbourhoods rather than straight rows. Streams, old trees, terrain and sacred sites would determine their placement. The village would therefore have:

winding stone lanes
stepping-stone shortcuts
small family courtyards
communal gardens
tiny bridges
houses built into or around slopes
covered walkways between closely related households
occasional wider plazas where several paths meet

The central parts nearest the temple would contain the oldest residences. Newer homes would extend outward along planned growth corridors, gradually merging with farmland, forest and specialist neighbourhoods.

Public spaces

The village contains courtyards, plazas, family gardens, canopy walkways, caravan plazas and starfield benchways.

Caravan plazas

These are broad open spaces near the East or West Gate where arriving groups can assemble without blocking narrow village lanes. They would accommodate carts, supplies, tents, visiting groups and ceremonial welcomes.

Family gardens

Small gardens surround or sit between homes. These are likely used for food, herbs, flowers and personal shrines rather than being purely decorative.

Canopy walkways

Raised bridges run through the forest canopy and possibly between elevated homes or neighbourhood terraces. They offer alternate pedestrian routes above wet ground and streams.

Starfield benchway

The Starfield Benchway consists of stone seating arranged in constellation patterns.

It is probably located in an open meadow or on a gentle rise with a wide view of the sky. By day it resembles an unusual network of seats and paths. At night, lanterns or path lights may reproduce a star map across the ground.

7. The communal buildings
Dining Hall

The Dining Hall is the centre of communal life and major celebrations.

Given the population, it is likely a very large hall or connected series of dining chambers rather than a small temple refectory. It would need:

long communal tables
flexible floor space
broad doors opening to outdoor plazas
raised or clear areas for announcements and ceremonies
access to the kitchen and stores
sheltered verandas for overflow seating
nearby paths broad enough for festival crowds

It is likely positioned between the sacred temple precinct and the residential village, making it accessible without placing daily noise directly beside the Inner Sanctuary.

Kitchen and stores

The kitchen and store complex supports ordinary meals, ritual cookware and festival preparation.

It likely lies immediately behind or beside the Dining Hall, with service access leading toward farmland and the West Gate. Underground or earth-cooled storage rooms could be built into the slope.

Bathhouse pavilion

The Bathhouse Pavilion contains separate male, female and communal wings.

It would logically stand near a strong water source downstream from the most sacred pools, possibly between the village and one of the waterfall-fed channels. The three wings could radiate from a common reception and changing area, each opening onto separate indoor and outdoor baths.

Training dojo

The dojo is used for soul-thread combat and veil-tuned martial forms. Nearby open grounds provide outdoor sparring and archery space.

This complex should sit toward the outer edge of the village, where training noise and projectiles do not interfere with homes or sacred gardens. The open grounds would need a long, relatively level field, likely on the plains-facing side of the valley.

Healing Lodge

The Healing Lodge provides alchemical treatment, physical recovery and veil-soothed rest.

It is likely surrounded by herbal gardens and running water. Quiet recovery rooms could face streams, woodland or enclosed courtyards. Its connection to the nursery and guest quarters would allow rescued arrivals to be assessed and stabilised without passing through the busiest public areas.

Guest quarters

Guest accommodation consists of suites facing forest and stream, with an attached or associated nursery wing for rescued children.

The suites are likely positioned near an entrance route but inside the strongest protections, allowing guests to be welcomed without immediately entering private family districts.

Nursery

The nursery has east-facing windows and soul-soothing lanterns and is protected by a truth-veil.

East-facing windows give it morning light. It is probably arranged around a protected garden or inner play court, with sleeping rooms deeper inside and supervised outdoor access toward the quieter garden paths.

8. Jayson’s private residence

The private family residence is a two-storey house shared by Jayson, Ayaka, Seika, Varethyn, Shion, Naru, FEI and Lysera.

It appears to stand on or near a ridge beside the temple settlement. A nearby high perch is used for night watch, suggesting the house occupies an elevated but still accessible position above the central village.

Ground floor

The front door opens directly into the dining room rather than a separate entrance hall. To the right is the lounge, which contains a sunken living firepit. Stairs rise on the left, and the kitchen lies farther beyond the dining area.

The lounge firepit is likely sunk below the main floor with seating arranged around it, allowing tails and multiple occupants to gather comfortably. Large openings or sliding panels probably connect the living area to the terrace and ridge view.

Upper floor

Upstairs are:

the shared master bedroom

The workshops are devoted to ritual weaving, engineering, and alchemy or general craft. The spare rooms can function as a map room, study or guest accommodation.

The shared bedroom and bath likely occupy the side with the best valley or sky view, while the workshops sit where noise, ventilation and access to storage are easiest.

9. Specialist neighbourhoods

The non-Kitsune residents are not scattered randomly through the village. Several groups have environments adapted to their species and culture.

Wolf-Kin den

A massive cavern very close to the village serves as the Wolf-Kin pack’s primary den.

“Very close” suggests that it is built into the valley wall or a low ridge rather than hidden in a remote mountain. A broad path would connect it to the residential district. The cave likely contains a large communal central space with smaller chambers branching from it.

Drakyn lava pit

Adjacent to the Wolf-Kin cavern is a stone lava pool surrounded by heat-pebble beds for the Drakyn.

This places the Drakyn area against the same rocky slope, probably where volcanic or engineered geothermal systems can be contained safely. The lava pit would be separated from ordinary wooden buildings by stone terraces, channels and fire-resistant barriers.

Neko sharehouse and Avian perches

The Neko live in a large communal sharehouse. Beside it are tree-like perches and lattice walkways for the Avians.

This neighbourhood would have strong vertical design:

broad communal rooms at ground level
exterior platforms
climbing routes
lattice bridges
elevated roosts
trees integrated into the architecture
sheltered landing or observation platforms

Its location is likely near the forest edge, where mature trees support the elevated infrastructure.

10. Agriculture

The primary farmland lies outside both the East and West Gates. Earlier Veil-generated farmland has been retired in favour of real-soil terraces.

The cultivated crops include:

rice
vegetables
herbs
fire-cherries
citrus

The fields are arranged in multi-level terraces irrigated by waterfalls and controlled watercourses.

Eastern farmland

The eastern fields are likely the more ceremonial and visually picturesque area because they sit near the honoured-guest approach. Rice terraces, flowering trees and citrus groves could frame the road approaching the East Gate.

Western farmland

The western fields would be more closely tied to supply movement, storehouses and the West Gate. Bulk crops, workshops, carts and agricultural storage would fit naturally here.

Herbal gardens supporting the Healing Lodge probably sit closer to the temple and lodge than the broad commercial fields. They provide incense plants, medicinal herbs, teas and ingredients for salves.

Emotionally aware drones monitor drought, grief and energy flow across the fields, while the obelisks coordinate irrigation. This means agricultural infrastructure is part technological, part spiritual and part ecological.

11. The southern harbour

The Grand Dock lies on Tsunejima’s southern shore. It is a deep-water harbour large enough for boats and small ships, built across massive harbour terraces rather than as one narrow wooden pier.

The harbour contains:

a harbour office

cargo lifts
beacon obelisks
ferry slips
a customs tea hall

The customs tea hall is particularly characteristic of Tsunejima. Formal processing and security occur in a hospitable setting rather than a sterile checkpoint.

Harbour Quarter

Beside the dock is a harbour quarter containing:

a wayhouse

guest barracks

This district acts as the island’s public front door. Visitors who are not yet cleared for the central valley can remain here, rest, trade or wait for escort.

12. Paths and transportation

Tsunejima’s path system operates on several levels.

Main roads

The principal road is the stone route between the Grand Dock and South Gate. Secondary roads likely connect the East and West Gates, farmland, village plazas and temple precinct.

These roads would be gently curved rather than ruler-straight because they follow streams, terraces and old trees.

Temple paths

Within the sacred grounds, paths become narrower and more ceremonial:

fitted stone paving
moss-edged stepping stones
covered timber galleries
small stream bridges
glowing thread markings
lineage tiles
lantern posts

The paths respond to vows and rites with soft light.

Residential lanes

Village lanes branch irregularly from the plazas and older temple routes. Many are footpaths rather than roads. Homes are connected to nearby obelisk nodes by waylines, giving each neighbourhood a subtle network of glowing guidance routes.

Elevated routes

Canopy bridges and lattice walkways provide routes above forest undergrowth, streams and steep terrain. These are especially prominent near the Avian neighbourhood and forest bands.

Hidden routes

The North-South forest trails and western hidden routes allow veilrunners, guards and trusted inhabitants to move discreetly around the settlement.

13. Natural landmarks
Threadgrove

The Threadgrove is used for heart-thread bonding.

It should be an old grove within walking distance of the temple but protected from daily traffic. Trees may grow in a natural ring or be connected by visible soul-thread effects during bonding rites.

Mossfield

The Mossfield is a quiet place for reflection and silent bonding.

It is probably an open meadow within the forest ring, cushioned by deep moss and sheltered from strong wind. It would provide one of the clearest views back toward the temple roofs or ridge.

Ember Circle

The Ember Circle is used for fire rites and rituals.

It likely occupies a stone clearing away from flammable woodland. A central ritual hearth would be surrounded by scorched or flame-marked stones and tiered standing or sitting areas.

Hidden shrine steps

Ancient shrine ruins stand beyond the active temple grounds and are reached by hidden steps.

These should feel older than the present settlement. The stairs may climb into the forested ridge, partially buried beneath moss, roots and fallen leaves. Their destination could overlook the original valley or descend into a forgotten sacred site.

Ridge house perch

Near the private residence is a high vantage point used for night watch.

This is distinct from the temple’s watch-perch balcony. The temple perch is part of the central sacred complex, while the ridge perch belongs to the private residential area.

14. Defences and island infrastructure

The island is protected by overlapping spiritual, technological and architectural systems.

Veil barriers

The hidden valley is protected by layered Veils that prevent discovery and entry without soul-thread permission. These barriers adapt to intent.

The WOK

The WOK stands in the central courtyard and rejects hatred or malice. It also supports truth-resonance during judgment.

Obelisk network

Smooth cylindrical obelisks, ten metres high and two metres wide, are distributed across the island’s biomes and shoreline. They perform several functions:

focus ley beams
reinforce Veil resonance
provide early warning
support truth-veil harmonics
assist weather control
act as signal beacons
provide non-lethal interdiction against hostile intent

Their description makes them visually distinct from the traditional temple architecture. Rather than disguising them completely, Tsunejima appears to embrace the contrast between ancient shrine forms and smooth monolithic constructs.

Veilwatch towers

The older watchtowers have been retuned for the island’s real-world environment and linked to constructs and beacon obelisks. Guardians include Veil-threaded spirits, soul-web detectors and emotion-sensitive warnings.

The tower network would be placed along:

ridge lines
gate approaches
harbour cliffs
biome boundaries
concealed forest routes
Construct systems

Island constructs regulate or assist with:

agriculture
weather
emotional pulse balance
spiritual residue cleansing
pathfinding lights
harbour convoys

This makes Tsunejima less a conventional island settlement and more a living integrated organism. Roads, water, wards, weather and communal emotion all feed into the same system.

15. What the temple home feels like

The temple home is not dominated by monumental emptiness. It is crowded with signs of life: children learning in courtyard circles, meals being prepared, fieldworkers moving through the gates, residents grooming tails on verandas, healers gathering herbs and lanterns shifting with the emotional current of those passing beneath them.

Morning begins with mist bells and pale light through the eastern nursery windows. Water can be heard almost everywhere: irrigation channels, koi streams, waterfall-fed ravines, bathhouse flows and narrow channels crossing beneath paths.

At midday, activity spreads toward the fields, dojo, workshops and plazas. The main shrine remains a visible anchor above the lower roofs, while the WOK rises far higher than every nearby structure.

At twilight, the obelisks begin to hum during communal meditation. Glowplate lanterns appear along house fronts. Waylines and vow-paths form fine ribbons of light through the village.

At night, the settlement becomes a bowl of scattered warm lights surrounded by dark forest. Above the trees, the WOK and ridge perches remain visible. Farther out, the larger obelisks mark mountains, shorelines and biome boundaries like a second, artificial constellation.

Important canon tension

The consolidated file describes six Kitsune women as sharing ownership with Jayson, but separately lists Lysera among the occupants of the private residence. That indicates Lysera lives within the family home but is not included in that particular ownership sentence.

The biome list also contains eight environmental features or regions, despite earlier campaign memory referring to five biomes. The cleanest interpretation is that five are principal climate biomes, while rivers, ravines and coastal cliffs are major terrain systems rather than additional climate zones.
